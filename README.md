# honey extension: what it really does at checkout, what it costs you in tracking, and how to stack cashback that pays out as money

Open the Chrome Web Store listing for Honey today and you'll see a number most people don't expect. The extension that once sat on more than 20 million browsers is now listed at around 12 million Chrome users, with roughly 4.4 million more on Edge. The product still works. The enthusiasm around it doesn't.

So the question behind most "honey extension" searches isn't really "what is Honey." It's three narrower questions: does it still find working codes, is it safe to leave installed, and is there something that pays back in actual money instead of gift cards? Those are the three things worth answering properly, because the honest answer to each is different.

## What Honey actually does when you shop

Honey is a free browser extension, made by PayPal, that runs coupon codes against your cart. You shop the way you normally would; when you land on a supported store the little "h" in your toolbar lights up, and at checkout you click Apply Coupons. Honey tries whatever codes it has for that merchant and applies the biggest discount that works. PayPal's own help documentation puts store coverage at 30,000+ sites across Chrome, Firefox, Safari, Edge and Opera, with a mobile app covering the rest.

Three features sit alongside the coupon testing:

- **Droplist** — you save a product and Honey watches its price for 30, 60 or 120 days, emailing you when it drops past your threshold (5% by default, adjustable).
- **Amazon seller comparison** — on Amazon product pages it compares other sellers' prices, factoring in shipping and Prime status.
- **PayPal Rewards (the old Honey Gold)** — points on eligible purchases at participating stores. Per Honey's help pages, 1,000 points converts to a $10 gift card, and US users can also redeem points at PayPal checkout once their PayPal account is connected.

The price of entry is nothing. Honey is free, and it's funded the way every coupon tool is funded: when your purchase tracks to Honey, the merchant pays an affiliate commission. PayPal disclosed this openly after March 2025, when Google updated Chrome Web Store policy to require it.

## The three limits that matter more than the feature list

**It does not promise the best available code.** Honey's own terms say the extension "may not always find you the best deal" and that the company isn't responsible for missed savings. That sentence exists for a reason. It came up repeatedly in the December 2024 investigation by the YouTuber MegaLag, which alleged Honey surfaced codes that suited its merchant partners rather than the deepest discount publicly available. Those allegations are the subject of a class action filed by creators against PayPal in January 2025.

**The rewards are not cash.** Points redeem for gift cards at 1,000, or through PayPal for eligible US users. If you want money in a bank account, Honey was never built for that. Some stores in Honey's network don't participate in PayPal Rewards at all — you'll see a trophy icon next to the stores that do.

**The checkout pop-up can quietly cost you money.** This is the part almost nobody talks about. Affiliate credit follows the last marketing link clicked before you pay. A coupon extension that pops up at the payment step and injects its own referral takes credit for the sale — and if you'd routed the purchase through a cashback platform, that's your cashback gone. ShopBack's own stacking guide flags coupon extensions as a common cause of broken tracking, and recommends using only codes shown on the merchant's ShopBack page.

> Two tools that both claim to save you money can cancel each other out at the exact moment you check out. The order you do things in decides which one pays.

## Where Honey stands after the 2024 fallout

The behaviour shown in that investigation has been changed. 9to5Google re-tested the extension in March 2025 and found that clicking "Got it" on Honey's pop-up no longer swapped the affiliate cookie — a change that lined up with Google's new extension policy. So the sneaky version is gone, and the extension is still safe to run from the official source.

What didn't come back is the trust, or the users. The Chrome Web Store listing now shows about 12 million users, down from a peak in the twenty-million range. That's the real state of play: Honey remains one of the biggest coupon tools, and a meaningful slice of its former user base now keeps a cashback extension alongside it, or instead of it.

## Coupons and cashback are two different jobs

Here's the distinction that most comparison articles blur. A coupon tool works at the last second, on the store you already chose. It tries to lower the number at checkout. A cashback platform works before that — it records your referral, then pays you a share of the commission the merchant pays for the sale.

They're funded by different parties, which is why they can stack:

1. The **sale price**, funded by the merchant's pricing.
2. The **coupon**, funded by the merchant's marketing budget at checkout.
3. The **cashback**, funded by the merchant's affiliate commission.
4. The **card reward**, funded by your card issuer.

None of those draw on the same dollar. Fail to activate cashback before you shop, though, and layer three never appears — which is exactly why the stack order matters.

## The order that keeps both working

If you're going to run a coupon extension and a cashback platform at the same time, the sequence isn't optional:

1. Start at ShopBack and activate the shopping trip — from the site, the app, or the extension button while you're on the merchant's page.
2. Use codes ShopBack surfaces, or codes published by the merchant itself. Assume anything from a third-party coupon site will override your tracking.
3. If Honey pops up at the payment step, decline it or turn it off for that session. Accepting a coupon-extension prompt at checkout is the classic way to hand the commission — and your cashback — to someone else.
4. Pay in the same browser session, on the same device. Don't switch from laptop to phone mid-order.
5. Pay with whatever card gives you the best category rewards. Card rewards land regardless of everything above.

Skipping step 3 is where most people lose the money they thought they were stacking. A $3 code is not worth a 5% cashback payout on a $400 order.

## What the money side actually looks like

Cashback isn't instant, and any article that implies otherwise is selling something. Here's the real timeline, per ShopBack's own documentation:

| Stage | What it means | Typical timing |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Tracked | Order registered against your account | Usually within 48 hours |
| Pending | Merchant's return window is still open, amount can change | Days to weeks |
| Confirmed | Merchant verified the order as final | 30–90 days for retail; after the trip for travel |
| Withdrawable | Cash can be transferred out | $10 minimum; up to $300 per day in the US |

Withdrawals go to PayPal or a bank account, and processing typically takes up to 10 calendar days, occasionally longer. Pending cashback is not spendable, and it can shrink if you return part of an order.

Things that will genuinely kill a tracked purchase: leaving the session to visit another coupon or referral site, using an outside promo code, running an aggressive ad-blocker or tracking-protection setup that blocks the affiliate cookie, checking out inside a social app's in-app browser, and buying excluded items — gift cards, tax and shipping are commonly excluded. Each merchant page lists its own exclusions, and it's worth thirty seconds of reading before a big purchase.

One more limit to know about: a ShopBack account can expire after 12 months of inactivity, so if you sign up and forget about it for a year, don't expect the balance to sit there forever.
